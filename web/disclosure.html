Disclosure

Most kernel bugs are reported and fixed in public. This page sets out how that works, what it costs while a fix is on its way, and how we report inside it.

How a kernel bug is handled

Kernel development takes place on public mailing lists. The bug report, the discussion, the patch and its review are posted there and archived on lore, where anyone can read them. The fix then lands in the maintainer’s tree, reaches mainline at the next merge window, is backported to stable, packaged by a distribution, and installed when a machine is updated. Weeks at best, often months.

The severity of a bug decides how it is reported. Ordinary bugs go to the subsystem’s public list. Serious ones go to security@kernel.org, which is private, and the fix goes out as soon as it is ready. It can be held back 7 days, 14 at most, and only if the reporter asks.

Automated reporting

syzbot has fuzzed the kernel for years and reports what it finds to the subsystem lists automatically; a bug with a reproducer is posted there in public. The conventions are well established: a report carries the kernel commit, the config, the crash and a reproducer, and a maintainer can close it as a duplicate or as invalid, or ask to receive no more. We report the same way and follow the same conventions. If you would rather not receive ours, for one subsystem or at all, tell us and we will stop.

The window between report and fix

From the first mail until the fix is installed, the bug is public and the machines that have it are unpatched. Anyone reading the list has what the maintainers have. No disclosure process removes that window; it can only be kept short.

How we report

Keeping that window short is the part we can act on. Every bug is assessed before it goes anywhere: what the primitive actually gives an attacker, and what privileges are needed to reach it. Only low-severity bugs are reported in public, and anything more serious goes to the private channel, as the kernel’s reporting guidance suggests.

When a bug does go to a public list, the report carries everything needed to confirm and fix it without a round trip to us. Time to a patch is the part of the window we can shorten, and that is where the effort goes.

We also do not send everything we find at once. A report waiting in a backlog is a bug in the open that nobody has started on, so we watch how quickly reports are taken up and fixed, and send new ones as the earlier ones are dealt with.

Discussion is welcome

Concern about a bug being reported before it is fixed is reasonable, and we take it seriously. Holding one back has a cost of its own: it does not get fixed, and it stays available to anyone else who finds it. Neither option is free. Reporting to a public list and fixing it there is how the kernel has worked for decades, and it has proved an effective way to get bugs fixed.

AI-assisted bug finding is new, and it produces more reports than any subsystem has asked for. We welcome suggestions on how to improve the way we disclose them: write to co@bugs.sh.
